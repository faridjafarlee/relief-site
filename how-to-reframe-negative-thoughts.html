How to reframe a negative thought

Reframing is not talking yourself out of something, and it is not positive thinking. It is checking whether a thought is accurate, in writing, in five steps.

1. Catch the thought

Write it down in the words it actually arrived in. Not the tidied-up version — "I always ruin these", not "I have some concerns about my performance". The tidying is already a kind of avoidance, and the harsh original is the thing you need on the page.

Rate how distressing it feels, 0 to 10. You will want this number later.

2. Name the distortion

Most distressing thoughts are running one of about ten patterns: catastrophizing, all-or-nothing thinking, mind reading, fortune telling, emotional reasoning, and the rest. Naming which one is doing the work is most of the work.

A named pattern stops being a fact about the world and becomes a habit of thought — and a habit is a thing you can argue with.

3. Weigh the evidence, both ways

Two columns. What genuinely supports this thought, and what genuinely argues against it.

The order matters: do the supporting column first and honestly. Skipping to the rebuttal turns the exercise into an argument you are having with yourself, and you will know you rigged it. The point is to find out what is actually true, which means being willing to find that the thought has something to it.

This is the step that does the work, and it is the step people skip. It is also the reason nothing else can do it for you: the search is what changes how the next thought arrives, at 2am, with no app open.

4. Write a fairer version

Not a more positive version. A fairer one — a sentence that accounts for both columns.

Usually it is duller than either the original thought or the affirmation you might have reached for. "That went badly and I know why, and I have handled worse" is not inspiring. It is accurate, and accurate is what holds up tomorrow.

5. Check what changed

Rate the distress again.

It may not have moved. That is information, not failure. A thought that will not shift under evidence is often pointing at something real that needs an action rather than an argument — and knowing that is more useful than a number that went down because you wanted it to.

Anyone who promises you the number always falls is selling something.

Three mistakes that stop it working

  • Doing it in your head. Written down, the chain has to slow to the speed of a sentence. In your head it runs at the speed of fear.
  • Writing the rebuttal first. See step 3. If you already know the answer, you are not testing the thought, you are reassuring yourself, and it wears off.
  • Only doing it in a crisis. The technique is a skill, and skills are built when the stakes are low. Practising on a mildly annoying thought is what makes it available for a bad one.
