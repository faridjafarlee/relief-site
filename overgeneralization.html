Overgeneralization

Turning one event into a never-ending pattern.

Why it happens

Overgeneralization turns a single event into a sweeping rule about your future, your identity, or how life always goes.

The mind uses one painful data point as a shortcut, then ignores the exceptions and counterexamples that would make the conclusion more accurate.

In real situations

Professional

“That meeting went awkwardly. I always ruin important conversations.”

One difficult interaction cannot accurately summarize every important conversation you have had or will have.

Personal

“I got rejected once, so this is just how it always goes for me. Nothing ever works out.”

A painful event can be real without becoming a permanent rule. Frequency needs to be counted, not emotionally assumed.

What it sounds like

  • This went wrong, so nothing ever works for me.
  • I got rejected once, so I will always be rejected.
  • Today was hard, so this whole month is ruined.

Contain the Pattern

Shrink the story back down to the actual event instead of promoting it into a lifelong rule.

  • Isolate the Event — Describe what happened this time in this context rather than using words like always, never, or everything.
  • Count the Real Frequency — Ask how many times this has truly happened and look for concrete counterexamples that break the pattern.
  • Write a Time-Bound Conclusion — End with a sentence that keeps the conclusion local, specific, and tied to this moment rather than your identity or future.

Quick checks

  • Count actual occurrences.
  • Use this time instead of always.
  • Look for counterexamples.

Ask yourself: How many times has this actually happened?

A kinder version: This time was hard, but it does not define the full pattern.
