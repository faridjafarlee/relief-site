Labeling

Assigning a rigid, global, and negative characteristic to yourself or others, often based on a single event or specific behavior, rather than describing the action itself.

Why it happens

Labeling is an extreme form of overgeneralization. Instead of saying, “I made a mistake on this report,” you attach a global, negative label to yourself: “I am a failure.” This cognitive leap transforms a specific, isolated action into a rigid identity.

The brain seeks efficiency and labels act as cognitive shortcuts, rapidly categorizing complex human behavior into simple, binary terms. However, these shortcuts sacrifice accuracy, creating a distorted worldview.

In real situations

Professional

“I totally bombed that presentation. I am completely incompetent and shouldn't be in this role.”

Your performance on one specific task on a specific day does not erase your past successes or dictate global incompetence.

Personal

“He cut me off in traffic. He is an absolute monster who does not care about anyone else.”

He executed a poor or rude driving maneuver. You do not know his circumstances, state of mind, or overall character based on a three-second interaction.

What it sounds like

  • I made an error, so I am a failure.
  • They were rude, so they are a bad person.
  • I procrastinated, so I am lazy.

Dismantle the Pattern

Use these steps to separate a moment, behavior, or mistake from a total identity verdict.

  • Separate Action from Identity — Force yourself to describe the specific behavior rather than a label. Replace “I am a loser” with “I lost this specific game.”
  • Seek Nuance — Human beings are incredibly complex. Acknowledge that a single label cannot accurately encompass an entire person, including you.
  • Run the “Is It 100% True?” Test — Ask whether the label applies to 100% of the time in every situation. If you can find counterexamples, the label is too global to be accurate.

Quick checks

  • Describe behavior, not identity.
  • Add context to the event.
  • Use specific language over global labels.

Ask yourself: What happened specifically, without identity labels?

A kinder version: This was one behavior in one moment, not my whole identity.
