How to check whether the apps you recommend still exist

Written for whoever maintains the resource page — a counselling centre, a library guide, a charity, a private practice. It takes about twenty minutes and needs no software you do not already have.

The problem is that nothing tells you

When an app closes, nobody writes to the pages that recommend it. There is no notification, no redirect, and no obligation on anyone to propagate the news. A page written carefully in 2022 quietly becomes wrong in 2025 and looks exactly the same.

Worse, the usual safeguards miss it. A broken-link checker tests whether a URL returns a page — and a withdrawn app’s App Store listing very often still returns a page. The failure only appears after somebody installs the app and cannot get in.

Three distinct things can go wrong, and they need different fixes:

What happenedWhat the link doesRight fix
Withdrawn — pulled from the store 404, or redirects to the store front pageRemove, or replace
Moved — republished under a new ID Old ID dead, app very much aliveRelink, do not delete
Closed but listed — service ended, listing left up Looks perfectly healthyRemove, and say why

The middle row is the one that causes real damage. Deleting a working app because its old link died removes a resource that people needed.

Step 1 — test every link against Apple’s own lookup

Every App Store URL contains a numeric ID, the part after id. Apple publishes a free lookup endpoint that needs no key and no account. If it returns "resultCount":0, the listing is not available.

curl -s "https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=1060691513&country=us"

To do a whole page at once, pull the IDs out of it first:

curl -s https://example.edu/counseling/apps.html \
  | grep -oE 'id[0-9]{6,}' | sort -u \
  | while read i; do
      n=$(curl -s "https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?${i/id/id=}&country=us" \
          | grep -o '"trackName":"[^"]*"' | head -1)
      echo "${i}  ${n:-*** NOT AVAILABLE ***}"
    done

Two things that will mislead you

  • Check more than one storefront. Availability is per country. An app that returns nothing in country=us may be perfectly alive in gb or de. A single zero is not a conclusion.
  • A single zero can simply be a blip. The endpoint occasionally returns resultCount:0 for an app that is demonstrably on sale. Re-run it before you write anything down.

Step 2 — check your PDFs, because nothing else does

This is the single most useful thing on this page. Handouts, printable guides and orientation packs are where dead links survive longest, because no link checker crawls a PDF, and because a printed copy cannot be corrected at all.

In one counselling-centre handout examined for this page, 11 of its 30 App Store links were dead. The same organisation’s website was in far better shape. The PDF had simply never been part of any check.

You do not need a PDF library to look. App Store links sit in the raw bytes as /URI entries:

curl -s https://example.edu/handout.pdf | strings | grep -oE 'https?://[^ )]*apple[^ )]*'

Step 3 — find every page that links a dead app

Once you know an ID is dead, search for the ID itself, in quotes, in any search engine:

"id825099621"

This works far better than searching the app’s name, and it has no false positives by construction, because nobody writes an App Store ID anywhere except inside a link. One such search returned five separate institutions still linking the same withdrawn app.

If you run a large site, this is also the fastest way to audit yourself: add site:yourdomain.edu to the same query.

Step 4 — relink rather than delete, and mind the deadlines

The tidy reaction is to delete the entry. It is often the wrong one.

  • If the app moved, find the current listing by searching the app name in the App Store and confirm the developer matches. Then update the ID. The resource is still good; only the address changed.
  • If the app is closing but has not closed yet, do not just remove the page. People who wrote things in it may lose them. Leave the entry up briefly with a line telling them how to export, and remove it after the deadline. A user who never sees the notice loses their journal, and quietly deleting the page guarantees they never see it.
  • If the app is gone with no successor, say so plainly rather than substituting something that looks similar. “This app is no longer available” is more useful than a replacement nobody vetted.

A worked example that trips every trap at once

Virtual Hope Box is a coping-skills app for service members and veterans, published by the US Defense Health Agency. It appears on a great many institutional resource pages under the ID 825099621. Checked on 26 August 2026:

LookupResult
id825099621&country=usnot available
id825099621&country=gbnot available
id825099621&country=canot available
id825099621&country=auavailable — last updated 8 November 2018
App Store search, “Virtual Hope Box” Virtual Hope Box (new), id6477622019, same publisher, updated 22 April 2026

Three separate lessons fall out of one app:

  • Had you checked only the US store, you would have called it dead. It is not dead; it is withdrawn from most storefronts and still listed in Australia.
  • Had you checked only Australia, you would have called it fine. It resolves — to a build last touched in 2018. A link that returns a page is not the same as a maintained app.
  • The right fix is neither delete nor leave alone. A current, maintained listing exists under a different ID. Relink to it.

This is also why the search in step 3 matters. Every page still pointing at 825099621 is sending people to something withdrawn or eight years stale, and nobody has told any of them.

What this method cannot tell you

  • Whether an app is any good. This checks existence, not quality, evidence base or data handling. Those are separate questions and harder ones.
  • Whether a listing that resolves is still usable. An app can remain in the store while its service is closed to new users, which no automated check will catch. That one needs a human to read the developer’s own announcements.
  • Anything about Android. Google Play needs its own pass; the two platforms diverge, and an app can be alive on one and gone from the other.
