Fortune Telling

Predicting a negative outcome without considering other, more likely possibilities. The assumption is that things will inevitably go wrong.

Why it happens

This distortion operates by treating a negative prediction as a proven fact before it happens. It bypasses rational evidence-gathering and jumps straight to the conclusion of failure or catastrophe.

It often serves as a maladaptive defense mechanism: by anticipating the worst, the individual attempts to protect themselves from disappointment while ironically guaranteeing distress in the present.

In real situations

Professional

“I know I'm going to fail the presentation tomorrow. I'll stutter, and everyone will think I'm incompetent.”

The presentation has not occurred; past competence and preparation are being ignored in favor of a fearful prediction.

Personal

“If I go to the party, nobody will talk to me and I'll end up standing in the corner looking pathetic.”

Social outcomes are highly variable, dynamic, and not predetermined by internal anxiety.

What it sounds like

  • This presentation will go badly.
  • I will never be able to handle this role.
  • The meeting will be a disaster.

Challenge Protocol

A structured approach to dismantle the fortune-telling pattern and re-anchor in present reality.

  • Examine the Evidence — What tangible proof do you have that this negative outcome is guaranteed? Are there past examples where things went well?
  • Generate Alternatives — Force yourself to articulate three other possible outcomes: the best case, the worst case, and the most realistic case.
  • Decatastrophize — Even if the negative outcome occurs, how would you cope? Remind yourself of your resilience and flexibility.

Quick checks

  • Use language of uncertainty.
  • List past times your prediction was wrong.
  • Plan for one likely and one fallback scenario.

Ask yourself: What evidence supports this prediction today?

A kinder version: I cannot predict the future; I can prepare and adapt.
