The 10 cognitive distortions, explained

Cognitive behavioral therapy calls them thinking traps: habits of thought that feel like accurate reporting and are not. Naming the one you are in is most of the work.

All-or-Nothing Thinking

Seeing things in black and white with no middle ground.

Your brain compresses nuance into extremes. If it is not perfect, it feels like a failure.

What it sounds like

  • If I miss one deadline, I am terrible at my job.
  • If this conversation is awkward, the whole relationship is broken.
  • If I am anxious today, I am back to square one.

How to challenge it

  • Look for the middle outcome.
  • Replace always/never with sometimes.
  • Rate the situation 0-10 instead of pass/fail.

Ask yourself: What middle outcome am I skipping?

A kinder version: Even if this is imperfect, it can still be meaningful progress.

Catastrophizing

Believing that what has happened or will happen will be so awful and unbearable that you won't be able to stand it.

Catastrophizing involves anticipating the worst possible outcome, even when it is highly unlikely. It is an evolutionary alarm system that overshoots and treats fear as proof.

What it sounds like

  • If I make one mistake, everyone will lose trust in me.
  • This symptom means something is seriously wrong.
  • If I say no, this opportunity is gone forever.

How to challenge it

  • Ask for the most likely outcome.
  • Name one manageable next step.
  • Estimate probability, not fear intensity.

Ask yourself: What is the most likely outcome if I zoom out?

A kinder version: This is uncomfortable, not catastrophic. I can handle the next step.

Mind Reading

Assuming you know what others think about you.

You fill in other people’s private thoughts with negative assumptions and react as if those assumptions are facts.

What it sounds like

  • They were quiet, so they must think I sounded stupid.
  • My manager looked serious, so I am in trouble.
  • They did not reply quickly, so I annoyed them.

How to challenge it

  • Separate evidence from interpretation.
  • Generate two alternative explanations.
  • If needed, ask directly for clarification.

Ask yourself: What observable facts do I actually have?

A kinder version: I do not know what they think yet; I can check instead of guessing.

Fortune Telling

Predicting a negative outcome without considering other, more likely possibilities. The assumption is that things will inevitably go wrong.

Fortune telling treats a negative prediction as a proven fact before it happens. It bypasses rational evidence-gathering and jumps straight to the conclusion of failure or catastrophe.

What it sounds like

  • This presentation will go badly.
  • I will never be able to handle this role.
  • The meeting will be a disaster.

How to challenge it

  • Use language of uncertainty.
  • List past times your prediction was wrong.
  • Plan for one likely and one fallback scenario.

Ask yourself: What evidence supports this prediction today?

A kinder version: I cannot predict the future; I can prepare and adapt.

Emotional Reasoning

Treating feelings as facts.

Because you feel fear, guilt, or shame, the mind concludes the threat or fault must be objectively true.

What it sounds like

  • I feel incompetent, so I must be incompetent.
  • I feel anxious, so this must be dangerous.
  • I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong.

How to challenge it

  • Name the feeling and pause.
  • Ask what facts support the conclusion.
  • Treat emotion as a signal, not proof.

Ask yourself: What emotion is loud right now, and what are the facts?

A kinder version: My feeling is valid, and it is not the full evidence set.

Should Statements

Rigid rules about how things must be.

Strict internal rules create pressure, shame, and anger when real life does not match perfection.

What it sounds like

  • I should always be productive.
  • I should never need help.
  • They should know what I need without me saying it.

How to challenge it

  • Swap should with prefer.
  • Check whether the rule is realistic.
  • Choose compassionate standards.

Ask yourself: Is this rule humane and sustainable?

A kinder version: I prefer this outcome, but I can be flexible and still effective.

Labeling

Assigning a rigid, global, and negative characteristic to yourself or others, often based on a single event or specific behavior, rather than describing the action itself.

Labeling is an extreme form of overgeneralization. Instead of saying “I made a mistake on this report,” you attach a global, negative label to yourself: “I am a failure.” This cognitive leap transforms a specific, isolated action into a rigid identity.

What it sounds like

  • I made an error, so I am a failure.
  • They were rude, so they are a bad person.
  • I procrastinated, so I am lazy.

How to challenge it

  • Describe behavior, not identity.
  • Add context to the event.
  • Use specific language over global labels.

Ask yourself: What happened specifically, without identity labels?

A kinder version: This was one behavior in one moment, not my whole identity.

Discounting the Positive

Dismissing good things as flukes or irrelevant.

Positive evidence gets filtered out, so your self-view stays skewed toward the negative.

What it sounds like

  • They only praised me to be nice.
  • That success does not count; it was luck.
  • I did well, but anyone could have done it.

How to challenge it

  • Treat positive data as real evidence.
  • Track wins and effort, not only outcomes.
  • Ask what this says about your actual capabilities.

Ask yourself: What positive data am I deleting right now?

A kinder version: This success counts, even if it was not perfect.

Overgeneralization

Turning one event into a never-ending pattern.

One data point gets stretched into an identity or life pattern without enough evidence.

What it sounds like

  • This went wrong, so nothing ever works for me.
  • I got rejected once, so I will always be rejected.
  • Today was hard, so this whole month is ruined.

How to challenge it

  • Count actual occurrences.
  • Use this time instead of always.
  • Look for counterexamples.

Ask yourself: How many times has this actually happened?

A kinder version: This time was hard, but it does not define the full pattern.

Personalization

Believing that you are entirely to blame for something that wasn't entirely your fault, or conversely, blaming others for something that was your fault. It is the assumption of outsized responsibility for external events.

This distortion operates on a flawed mental model where the individual places themselves at the center of the universe. When adverse events occur, the immediate, automatic assumption is personal responsibility.

What it sounds like

  • The project slipped, so it is entirely my fault.
  • They seem upset, so I must have caused it.
  • The event went poorly because of me.

How to challenge it

  • Map all contributing factors.
  • Separate influence from responsibility.
  • Use fair accountability language.

Ask yourself: Which factors were outside my control?

A kinder version: I can own my part without carrying all the blame.
